Abstract 
The immediate spread of PV-systems in homes is commonly impeded by the intermittency 
of solar generation and the temporal mismatch between household demand and peak energy 
generation timing. Especially in the example of Sarawak, where substantial financial premiums 
occur at peak morning times due to certain Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs, deterministic battery 
controllers have not been effective to ensure economic efficiency as they are reactive and 
cannot capture future weather phenomena. To overcome the latter limitation, an AI-powered 
solar battery optimization model is introduced, aimed at an improvement in the operational 
dispatch of residential energy storage solutions. Taking a quantitative simulation perspective, 
a high-fidelity "Digital Twin" of a general Sarawakian household was designed (in Python) 
with a 5 kWp PV array and a 10 kWh Lithium-Iron-Phosphate (LFP) battery which was 
simulated with strict electrochemical conditions for simulating the domestic life. The core of 
the system intelligence is controlled by a Random Forest Regressor built on two years' worth 
of historic meteorological telemetry from Visual Crossing, which predicts solar irradiance 
twenty-four hours in advance. Such forecasting component powers a new type of conditional 
control logic to dynamically vary between a "Standard Self-Consumption" mode and a 
proactive "Conservation Mode," effectively hoarding stored energy during predictive overcast 
periods to offset expensive grid imports at high tariff windows. The analysis compared with a 
norm-based baseline indicates an improvement in economy and the new predictive approach 
for economically better energy self-sufficiency. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis verifies the 
stability of the optimization logic under different hardware configurations, providing the 
proposed framework as a credible, scalable solution for intelligent residential energy 
management in tropical climates.

1. INTRODUCTION 
1.1. Introduction 
Global efforts to combat climate change and improve energy security have accelerated the 
adoption of renewable technologies, with solar power emerging as a key solution due to its vast 
natural abundance. The most widely used solar energy technology is solar photovoltaics (PV), 
which are installed all over the world and are inexpensive. According to Ahmed et al. (2025), 
Solar photovoltaic (PV) power is currently the most widely used electricity source globally 
based on the number of installations due to the ongoing reduction in PV panel prices and the 
rise in solar cell efficiency. Even though solar photovoltaic (PV) systems are widely used and 
reasonably priced, their energy generation during daylight is exceedingly unpredictable as it is 
closely related to changing weather conditions. According to Soo et al. (2025), Solar radiation 
is an important factor influencing the efficiency of Photovoltaic (PV) power generation because 
it directly determines how much energy can be harnessed from the sun. The availability of solar 
radiation in tropical regions like Sarawak, Malaysia, is influenced by several factors, including 
cloud cover, humidity, and seasonal variations (Soo et al., 2025). This variability, combined 
with the sun's daily cycle, causes a mismatch between supply and demand.  Solar power is 
abundant at midday, but households use the most energy when the sun is low, especially in the 
mornings and evenings.  
 
Moreover, this mismatch becomes a major financial burden under Time-of-Use (ToU) 
tariffs, a pricing system where electricity costs significantly more during specific times of the 
day. Homeowners are compelled to buy expensive grid electricity in the evenings, when rates 
are at their highest, which reduces self-sufficiency and puts an "evening peak" strain on the 
2 
 
utility network. According to Muzmar et al. (2015), Malaysia is currently exploring the 
possibility of enacting a Time of Use Tariff (ToU) because the country's demand for power 
supplies has been rising dramatically in recent years. This transition is especially possible in 
Sarawak, where an operational framework for variable pricing already exists.  Sarawak Energy 
Berhad currently implements ToU tariffs for the commercial and industrial sectors, such as 
Tariff C3, making the extension of this pricing structure to residential users a logical and 
technically feasible step toward demand-side management. 
 
A hybrid renewable energy system (HRES), which combines various renewable energy 
sources and/or an energy storage system, can temporally balance the availability of renewable 
energy resources with the demand for electricity (Memon et al., 2022). The focal point of this 
analysis is the grid-tied hybrid solar battery system, which has established itself as a primary 
solution to the challenge of intermittency. By integrating photovoltaic generation with battery 
storage and utility network, this architecture enables the efficient capture of surplus solar 
energy produced during peak daylight hours, preserving it for consumption during periods of 
low generation rather than wasted. In Sarawak, this core solar-plus-battery technology is 
already a proven and critical component of the Sarawak Alternative Rural Electrification 
Scheme (SARES). SARES, an innovative government-community initiative implemented by 
Sarawak Energy, provides 24-hour electricity to households in remote villages via solar or 
micro-hydro solutions (Chong et al., 2020). While initiatives like SARES operate off-grid to 
ensure basic reliability, applying this same technology to a grid-tied residence fundamentally 
shifts the objective toward economic optimization. For homeowners connected to the utility 
network, hardware alone is insufficient; simply having a battery does not guarantee financial 
savings.

  A paradigm shifts to optimize the choice between storing energy or drawing energy from 
the grid is required. We need more than hardware that reacts to current conditions; we need a 
smart system that proactively anticipates future requirements. In recent years, Artificial 
Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) have become game-changing technologies that 
can address optimization problems grounded in stochastic environmental data. According to 
Mahjabeen and Mohammad (2023), AI systems can analyze vast data, including historical 
weather systems and the radiation of the sun, as well as geographical information to estimate 
the solar resources. AI models predict solar power production by analyzing historical and real 
data like weather predictions, energy consumption, or market prices (Mahjabeen & 
Mohammad, 2023). In line with this technical progress, we put forward the idea of an AI-driven 
solar battery optimization solution. It applies to a machine learning model, which has been 
trained on the historical weather data of Sarawak, to forecast the hourly solar power generated 
for the upcoming day. An optimization algorithm is generated from these forecasts to provide 
a 24-hour battery charging schedule to minimize electricity costs while providing data-centric 
solutions to energy management through the provision of an interactive Streamlit dashboard. 
 
1.2. Problem Statement 
The fundamental technical challenge hindering the efficiency of grid tied hybrid solar 
battery systems in Sarawak is the stochastic nature of solar irradiance combined with the lack 
of predictive control logic in residential inverters. In tropical climates like Sarawak, high 
frequency cloud cover and seasonal monsoon variations create severe fluctuations in power 
generation known as intermittency (Soo et al., 2025). This creates a temporal mismatch 
between peak generation during midday and peak residential demand in the evening which

Current commercial Battery Management Systems (BMS) are based on “Deterministic 
Reactive Control" or "Self-Consumption” logic (Pan et al., 2022). These systems are based on 
strict, static rules, meaning they charge when solar exceeds load and discharge when load 
exceeds solar. The shortcoming of such an approach is that it is myopic: it responds only to the 
present situation without consideration of an immediate future state (Notton et al., 2018). An 
example would be if a reactive controller discharges the battery at 04:00 AM to support a small 
load when electricity is cheap in the off-peak period, thereby depleting the reserves required to 
support the high-priced peak tariff window starting at 07:00 AM without realizing it (Pan et al., 
2022). Moreover, traditional timer-based scheduling is insufficient, as it cannot adapt to 
variable daily weather conditions; for example, a fixed schedule to discharge at night would 
fail if the battery never charged due to an unexpected afternoon storm (Notton et al., 2018). 
 
This lack of Look Ahead capability results in suboptimal energy dispatch where stored 
renewable energy is wasted on low value periods forcing the homeowner to import expensive 
grid power during peak hours. There is a distinct lack of research on integrating probabilistic 
machine learning forecasts trained specifically on Sarawak’s unique microclimate into a rule-
based optimization framework. Consequently, a technical gap exists in determining how 
accurate AI driven forecasting must be to enable a residential system to actively arbitrate energy 
prices and outperform standard non predictive controllers.

1.4.3. Phase 3: Iteration (or Construction) 
In this phase the software is constructed in iterative, 'Sprints' or short work cycles. This 
phase will be broken into several increments. The first foundational sprint shall be dedicated 
to coding and testing the Minimum Viable Product (MVP) of the 'Virtual Home' environment: 
the Python classes for the battery, predefined load profiles, and simulated Time-of-Use (ToU) 
grid tariff. The next increment of creating the AI forecaster will be very iterative. A Random 
Forest Regressor (RFR)-based baseline model will be coded and tested. The selected model is 
a powerful model, as it is used for numerical prediction problems, such as predicting kilowatts. 
According to Ouedraogo et al. (2018), RFR offers several advantages over traditional methods: 
it is nonparametric, it is highly accurate and highly predictive, and it identifies variable 
importance. It is particularly useful as an estimator to model the non-linear, complex and 
dynamic relationships among the weather inputs (such as solar irradiance as well as cloud 
cover) and the output from the final power generation, which is where the forecasting starts to 
be very useful. A benchmark accuracy (MAE/RMSE) will be calculated based on these tests. 
Results of such tests will be used to advise on the next sprint; for instance, if accuracy is too 
low and not as it should be, provided by these tests. The subsequent cycle will code in new 
features such as “hour” and “day_of_year” and retrain the model, performing the loop and 
training again until accuracy is adequate. This will be followed by the construction and testing 
of the rule-based optimizer together with the simulation. Finally, we will implement the 
Streamlit dashboard iteratively by prototyping one or the other of the app features, then 
piecewise adding the complex energy flow and battery charts, as this will help us build the 
dashboard.

1.5. Scope 
This project's scope is strictly limited to the design, development, and simulation of an 
optimization system within a Python environment. The system will use predefined 24-hour

residential load profiles and historical weather data from a third-party API, Visual Crossing, 
for a single representative Sarawak location, Kuching, to digitally model a grid-tied solar 
battery system. Solar power generation will be simulated using the pvlib library. The main

2.2.2. The Challenge of Stochastic Intermittency 
The inherent stochastic and intermittent nature of solar energy presents a significant 
challenge for electrical grid integration because photovoltaic (PV) generation fluctuates 
randomly across time scales ranging from seconds to seasons (Notton et al., 2018). Unlike 
conventional power sources, solar output is heavily dependent on variable environmental 
factors like cloud cover and daily cycles, making it difficult for grid operators to maintain the 
mandatory real-time balance between electricity production and consumption. This 
unpredictability forces utility managers to maintain expensive energy reserves and keep backup 
generators running at inefficient levels to immediately compensate for sudden drops in solar 
power. Consequently, this requirement for additional flexibility and specific reserves to handle 
unexpected ramp rates significantly increases the operational costs and technical complexity 
of integrating high levels of PV penetration into the standard power grid.

2.3. Hybrid Solar Battery Systems as a Solution 
2.3.1. System Architecture and Functionality 
A hybrid renewable energy system solves the stochastic intermittency of solar power by 
providing a complete solution by combining the generation of solar power through photovoltaic 
technology with battery energy storage and utility grid connections to ensure uninterrupted 
electric power supplies to end users. As a result, this architecture is based on the battery energy 
storage system, which absorbs solar energy in high solar irradiation periods and releases 
electric energy when generation has dropped or demand has increased (León Gómez et al., 
2023). Having a connected hybrid network for system configuration enables effective 
management of the power flow between direct current solar panels and battery on the one hand 
and alternating current utility grid on the other hand. This is a combination which not only 
stabilizes the voltage and frequency, on top of that makes electricity supply more efficient and 
reliable compared to monolithic electricity systems.  
 
To materialize this tactic, an architecture consists of the 4 major parts of the architecture: 
PV panels, battery storage unit, DC to AC inverter, and utility connection represented in Figure 
2.2. In this topology, this photovoltaic PV system generates direct current electricity which 
goes directly into either the storage reservoir or directly into the inverter (Gabr et al., 2021). 
The inverter is then converted direct current from solar arrays and batteries into alternating 
current, applicable to home load and acts as the hub for the conversion. This energy is then fed 
on an AC busbar that connects to the national grid in bidirectional fashion, enabling the system 
to smoothly import power when there is slack in internal generation or return surplus energy to 
the provider of electricity.

detailed in the provided schematic. As illustrated in figure 2.3, energy is captured by solar

battery bank to ensure optimal charging and storage (Sharma & Singh, 2018). The inverter 
serves as the critical bridge, drawing power from the batteries or panels to provide electricity 
to the house. Furthermore, the system is integrated with the utility grid via a meter, which 
monitors the bidirectional flow of energy, allowing for grid-tied operation where excess power 
can be exported or supplemental power can be drawn.

2.4. Economic Drivers: From Reliability to Optimization 
2.4.1. The Paradigm Shift: Grid-Tied Economic Optimization 
With the urbanization of Sarawak, adaptation of proven hybrid solar technology for grid 
connected residential applications is crucial. The main operational target in this scenario will 
not be energy sufficiency but rather economic optimization. Standalone systems such as 
SARES focus on continuous power supply whereas urban grid connected systems must grapple 
with financial constraints by avoiding expensive electricity imports during peak pricing hours. 
This shift requires a change from backup strategies to smart dispatch algorithms optimized to 
minimize costs. 
 
2.4.2. The Evolution of Pricing Mechanisms (Tariff Structures) 
The power tariff’s structure, which dictates the cost of energy consumption at different 
times of day, plays a key role in the economic optimization of grid-connected systems. As a 
model, Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs introduce a dynamic pricing model to electricity grid-
connected systems, with prices that vary at different times of day according to usage patterns 
and demand. When compared to the traditional flat rate tariff system, the former uses a dynamic 
pricing model under which the price of electricity in a power grid reflects these varying charges. 
Using a ToU system, the day is segmented into periods when prices are significantly higher 
during peak hours, as the demand for electricity is greatest, and substantially lower during off-
peak hours when consumption drops (Pan et al., 2022). This dynamic mechanism generates 
effective price signals to encourage consumers to shift usage away from expensive periods and 
transforms the battery from a passive backup to an active financial instrument capable of 
executing energy arbitrage by storing low-cost energy and discharging it during high-cost 
windows.

composition of domestic tariffs to establish a Time-of-Use (ToU) scheme designed to raise

2.5. Machine Learning for Solar Forecasting 
2.5.1. The Shift to Data-Driven Forecasting 
To transition solar power forecasting from an underlying theory of solar generation to an 
actual forecast driven by data-driven methods, these advanced algorithms will replace the 
primitive and static models, paving the way for the generation of new solutions. According to 
Long et al. (2014), these computational methods for analysis of real-time solar radiation are 
better than those that use the traditional weather-based techniques as they make use of the 
already established weather data, namely temperature and insolation, which are found most 
critical, resulting in forecasts at much lower error rates. In contrast to the previous persistent 
approach which merely assumes that the predicted future solar output will be equal to the 
current state, the data-driven techniques utilize the patterns in the training data for learning to 
minimize the Root Mean Square Error and serve as a more stable grid management solution. 
So, such algorithms as the Artificial Neural Network can produce the most accurate prediction 
for immediate daily forecast, confirming the effectiveness of incorporating ML in renewables 
planning.

2.5.2.1. Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) 
For accurate prediction of solar radiation, Artificial Neural Networks, and the multilayer 
perceptron model using feed-forward back-propagation as its underlying theory, can be an 
invaluable tool to model the intricate, non-linear relationships (Notton et al., 2019). As evident 
from Figure 2.5, the core architecture has been constructed in parallel such that the input layer 
receives meteorological factors, the hidden layer processes the information with synaptic 
weights, and the output layer supplies the predicted solar irradiation figures. Each neuron 
operates in this system by computing a weighted sum of inputs while applying the 
corresponding activation function (sigmoid for the hidden layer and linear for the output layer) 
to find the level of signal passing to the next level. To find the best tradeoff between its 
performance and computational cost in the optimization of accuracy, the network design often 
is composed of having only one hidden layer and it takes advantage of training algorithms such 
as Levenberg-Marquardt in order to constantly modify the connection weights to obtain the 
lowest prediction error.

2.5.2.2. Support Vector Regression (SVR) 
Support Vector Regression further extends the classification prowess of Support Vector 
Machines to the regression domain, providing a strong framework for solar power forecasting 
in nonlinear weather scenarios utilizing the kernel trick (Abuella & Chowdhury, 2017). Unlike 
traditional regression methods that minimize the error between the predicted and actual values 
for all data points, in SVR there is a designated insensitive region around the regression 
function where errors falling within this region are ignored if they fall within a defined 
threshold ϵ. As depicted in Figure 2.6, this architecture constructs a boundary margin that 
envelops the regression line; only data points falling outside this "tube" serve as the input to 
the cost function, which is minimized by balancing the model's complexity against these 
deviations. As a result of mapping input vectors into a higher dimensional feature space, SVR 
converts nonlinear relationships into linear ones, thereby providing a global and unique 
solution to overcome overfitting problems faced in commonly used neural networks.

2.5.2.3. Random Forest Regressor (RFR) 
Random Forest Regression is built on the robust ensemble of learning architecture of 
Bootstrap Aggregating, which allows performance to be increased with more decision trees 
built in the training stage (Babu et al., 2025). The model achieves this as shown in Figure 2.7 
by building multiple bootstrapped subsets of the original dataset and training a separate 
decision tree on each subset at the same time rather than through sequential training. In this 
structure each tree splits the data independently by optimizing the mean square error to 
minimize variance and capture complex data patterns. A final forecast output is obtained from 
the average of all individual predictions, thus effectively reducing the overfitting risk often 
associated with individual tree models, as well as ensuring high accuracy for solar generation 
and demand forecasting.

2.6.2.1. Reinforcement Learning (RL) 
Reinforcement Learning refers to a data-driven optimization approach: an intelligent agent 
learns to make decisions through continuous interaction with a dynamic environment (Liu & 
Gou, 2025). As seen here in the Figure 2.8 Reinforcement Learning schematic, the architecture 
becomes a closed loop involving state observation, action execution, and reward calculation. 
In the specific context of battery optimization, the agent watches the current state of a system 
(𝑆𝑡), typically consisting of multidimensional variables such as real time photovoltaic 
generation, household load demand, and battery’s State of Charge. To do so, the agent creates 
a controlled action (specific action space) to have the battery charge, discharge or remain idle 
for some period. After completing this action, the environment gives feedback in terms of a 
numerical reward (rt), reflecting how well the decision was executed in decreasing its 
dependence on the grid or costs. During successive iteration, the agent constantly updates its 
policy network to maximize the sum reward, thereby learning the best (and most efficient) 
31 
 
energy dispatch control strategy without relying on a fixed, pre-defined mathematical model 
of the physics of the building. 
 
Figure 2.8: Architecture of RL (Liu & Gou, 2025) 
 
2.6.2.2. Rule Based Control (RBC) 
Rule Based Control serves as a deterministic optimization strategy in which predetermined 
logical sequences guide control of energy flow (Liu & Gou, 2025). Architecture follows a 
hierarchical decision-making structure, as illustrated with Rule Based Control logic flowchart 
in Figure 2.9. It starts with an Input layer, where real-time system variables such as photovoltaic 
generation and load demand are ingested. This data then flows through to a central conditional 
node, represented by the diamond symbol, which compares the current state with specific 
thresholds or Boolean conditions. Based on this appraisal, the logic splits into separate paths 
where certain Calculations must be performed to determine the exact power requirements, for 
example, how much surplus solar energy there is to be had. Following this quantitative step, a 
Process phase is taken. Then the control command is formalized for finalization, and the actions 
it carries out, such as charging the battery or transferring power to the grid, happen. That 
32 
 
sequential "If Then" system ensures that operation of the battery is strictly aligned with 
established priority rules at each step (without necessity for further learning). 
 
Figure 2.9: Architecture of RBC (Liu & Gou, 2025) 
 
2.6.3. Comparative Analysis 
Selecting the appropriate control architecture requires a holistic evaluation that moves 
beyond theoretical potential to examine empirical operational performance. To determine the 
optimal strategy for a residential energy management system, it is essential to benchmark the 
candidates against tangible engineering constraints including energy efficiency, grid stability, 
and computational transparency. The following analysis synthesizes experimental data from 
Liu and Gou (2025), who conducted a rigorous comparative study between a stochastic 
Reinforcement Learning model and a deterministic Rule Based Controller under identical 
environmental conditions. This evaluation separates the performance outcomes into 
quantitative economic metrics and qualitative operational assessments to fully justify the 
algorithmic selection. 
 
33 
 
2.6.3.1. Quantitative Performance Metrics 
To rigorously evaluate the economic efficiency of the control strategies, Liu and Gou 
(2025) conducted a comparative study using real world residential load data. The primary 
metric for assessment was the Energy Self Sufficiency Rate (SSR), which measures the 
percentage of household demand met by onsite solar and storage. The empirical results 
demonstrated that the Rule Based Control strategy achieved a superior Self Sufficiency Rate 
of 94%, whereas the Reinforcement Learning model using Proximal Policy Optimization 
reached a lower peak of 86%. This performance gap highlights that the deterministic logic of 
the Rule Based Control is more effective at maximizing the utilization of stored energy. 
Furthermore, regarding grid dependency, the study highlighted that in specific test scenarios 
(Case 1), the Rule Based Control successfully eliminated grid reliance entirely by reducing 
grid imports to 0.00 kWh. In contrast, the Reinforcement Learning agent still required 4.21 
kWh of imported electricity to meet the same load demand, indicating a lower efficiency in 
mitigating costs during critical periods. 
 
2.6.3.2. Qualitative Operational Metrics 
And, beyond sheer numerical efficiency, the control strategy stability operationally is 
crucial for battery health as well as grid compliance (Liu and Gou, 2025). The study found a 
significant qualitative difference in the power dispatch profiles produced for the two models. 
It is in this case that the Reinforcement Learning model has clearly observed distinct sawtooth 
variations between charging and discharging profiles, due to the stochastic randomness 
inherent in its strategy exploration process. These abrupt and unpredictable switching processes 
can cause fluctuations in local grid voltages, leading to fast physical deterioration of battery 
cells. In contrast, the Rule Based Control maintained a smoother and more continuous 
34 
 
operation profile, ensuring that the battery discharge frequency is consistent with the peak loads 
without irrelevant perturbations. In addition, when it comes to system clarity, Reinforcement 
Learning performs as a black box model with unfathomable decision logic for the system 
operator. Alternatively, Rule Based Control provides an interpretable and logical paradigm 
which enables engineers to check the nature of their decision on when to dispatch equipment 
and verify that compliance with safety constraints is rigorously maintained.

As seen in Table 2.2, the comparative evidence, the Rule Based Control approach is chosen

sure that all control decisions are explainable and verifiable, thus preventing the opacity issue 
(the problem of black box in Reinforcement Learning). Thus, Rule Based Control ensures a

required for a reliable Home Energy Management System.

2.7.2. System A: Off Grid Reliability vs. Grid Tied Economics 
In the autonomous energy systems domain, Babu et al. (2025) provided a comprehensive 
framework titled "Forecasting and optimization of a residential off-grid solar photovoltaic-
battery energy storage system." They specialize in the context of Bangladesh, where the aim is 
36 
 
to realize full energy independence from the national utility grid. To do this, they have proposed

operational goal is reliability, specifically ensuring that the solar PV and battery capacities are 
sufficiently large to sustain load continuity without any external grid support.

However, a critical divergence exists between their approach and the requirements of an 
urban residential setting in Sarawak. The framework proposed by Babu et al. (2025) operates 
under a paradigm of Energy Constraints, where the system must be oversized to guarantee 
survival during low generation periods. For instance, their optimization logic resulted in scaling 
the photovoltaic capacity from 11 kW to 30 kW and deploying a massive 120 kWh battery to 
ensure autonomy. This approach effectively treats the battery as a static backup reservoir rather 
than an active financial asset.

In contrast, the proposed Sarawak simulation operates under a paradigm of Financial

ignores electricity prices to focus on availability, this project assumes the grid is always

here is the transition from Passive Reliability Sizing to Active Economic Dispatch. While Babu 
et al. utilize Random Forest to determine how big the system must be to avoid blackout, this 
project utilizes Random Forest to determine when a fixed size system should discharge to 
minimize costs. This distinction highlights the necessity for a control logic that prioritizes price 
arbitrage over simple state of charge preservation, addressing the specific economic needs of 
grid tied prosumers that are not covered by off grid reliability models.

of their innovation is a three-step deterministic algorithm, as illustrated in the flowcharts of 
their methodology (Figures 2.11, 2.12, and 2.13), which sequentially calculates the energy 
availability, determines the target energy exchange, and finally schedules the specific battery 
charging or discharging intervals. This "Rule Based" approach was proven to be approximately 
30 times faster than equivalent linear programming solvers while maintaining similar economic 
performance.

3.2. General Research Framework 
3.2.1. System Architecture 
The research employs a quantitative Digital Twin framework designed to mimic the 
behavior of a residential hybrid solar system within a virtual environment. As illustrated in 
Figure 3.2, the system architecture is constructed as a closed loop simulation composed of two 
primary functional layers known as the Offline Training Pipeline and the Online Operational 
Simulation. 
 
Figure 3.2: Proposed architecture 
 
The Offline Training Pipeline serves as the developmental backbone of the system and is 
responsible for synthesizing the intelligence required for predictive control. Structurally, this 
layer acts as a static repository that integrates three critical modules starting with the Data 
Ingestion Module. This high-fidelity database contains multi variable meteorological records 
51 
 
including Global Horizontal Irradiance, Direct Normal Irradiance, Diffuse Radiation, 
Temperature, Humidity, Cloud Cover, and Precipitation sourced from the Visual Crossing API. 
Integrated with this database is the Physics Engine, a computational layer powered by the pvlib 
library which functions as a virtual solar array to map weather inputs to theoretical power 
outputs thereby establishing the ground truth for training. The final pillar of this layer is the 
Machine Learning Core where the Random Forest Regressor is encapsulated to absorb the 
processed data and produce a serialized model file that effectively freezes the learned weather 
energy correlations for deployment. 
 
Encompassing this development layer is the Online Operational Simulation that serves as 
an active runtime environment to simulate the real-time dynamics of energy flow. The layer is 
a part of the Digital Twin proper, it involves three interacting components, which start with the 
Forecasting Engine. This module acts as a runtime instance of the pretrained Random Forest 
(RF) model and produces continuous twenty-four-hour predictions from live weather streams. 
These predictions are then passed to the Decision Controller, a rule-based logic unit which is 
the brain of the system, cross-referencing energy forecasts against the Sarawak Tariff C3 
schedule to optimize dispatch commands. Ultimately, the Virtual Hardware Layer takes these 
commands using mathematical state space models to capture the physical limitations of the 
battery and the hybrid inverter. This combination provides the offline layer that generates 
predictive capability and the online layer which performs the control strategy.

3.3. Data Acquisition and Preprocessing 
The quality of the data is always the key to the integrity of this method, since it involves 
simulation rather than hardware deployment. The actual data collection and acquisition process 
is designed to generate a high-fidelity digital environment that simulates the actual 
meteorological conditions of Sarawak. This stage entails four key vectors: meteorological data 
sourcing, systematic methods of data cleaning, the physics-based synthesis of solar generation 
profiles, and an Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) protocol to statistically validate these inputs. 
 
3.3.1. Meteorological Data Sourcing 
Thus, this predictive model is based on the utilization of a high level of realistic 
meteorological data on the tropical climate of Sarawak. Historical weather telemetry in this 
study was obtained through Visual Crossing Weather Data Services API. An academic license 
was formally requested and granted to access advanced radiative parameters essential for solar 
54 
 
engineering, thereby upgrading the account level to a premium student tier. With this 
permission, our dataset was finally obtained, and it includes a continuous two-year span from 
January 1, 2023, to January 1, 2025, for the coordinates of Kuching, Sarawak (Latitude 1.55° 
N, Longitude 110.36° E). This multi-year period is important so that we can get the full seasonal 
variability including the Northeast Monsoon and inter-monsoon phases where solar generation 
profiles are mostly affected. This premium access allowed me to retrieve a granular feature 
vector combining GHI with the more accurate Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI) and Diffuse 
Horizontal Irradiance (DIF). The three parameters present a complete characterization of the 
solar geometry necessary for physics-based modeling. Apart from the radiation data, ambient 
temperature, relative humidity, cloud cover and precipitation levels were also recorded to act 
as environmental predictors for the machine learning algorithm. 
 
3.3.2. Data Preprocessing and Cleaning 
After acquiring the raw meteorological dataset, a systematic preprocessing pipeline is 
applied to ensure data integrity and temporal consistency prior to ingestion into the simulation 
environment. As presented in Figure 3.4, the process of transformation starts with the raw 
Comma-Separated Values (CSV), which is made up of 17,568 hourly observations, which is 
subject to a timestamp parsing process, which converts the ISO-8601 formatted date-time 
strings into Python datetime objects. Time zone localization is an essential step in this phase. 
Since the calculations on the solar geometry are made on the position of the sun precisely with 
the local longitude, the dataset index is properly localized to Asia/Kuching time zone (UTC+8). 
This avoids alignment errors that would cause peak solar irradiance to be incorrectly shifted by 
an offset of eight hours from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC).  
 
55 
 
After temporal alignment, the dataset is subjected to data validation and data imputation to 
correct any possible discontinuities inherent in remote sensing telemetry. The time series is 
then resampled to a strict hourly frequency such that missing rows or irregular intervals are 
identified. While the Visual Crossing dataset is of good quality, there may be transmission gaps, 
so a linear interpolation method is performed to fill minor discontinuities in an interval not 
exceeding two hours in its duration based on the assumption of a gradual response to variations 
in weather. For existing gaps that exceed this threshold, forward-fill can be applied to fill in the 
last observed data. Last, a physical limit check is executed across the radiative and precipitation 
columns to ensure non-negativity, which could have been corrected due to sensor noise giving 
inaccurate negative values for Global Horizontal Irradiance or rainfall.

3.3.3. Synthetic Generation of Ground Truth 
The creation of the predictive model faced a major challenge while working with the 
problem of not having historical power generation logs from a real installation at the site under 
consideration. To bridge this gap, we used Physics-Based Synthesis to learn a theoretical 
“Ground Truth” target variable from the labeled dataset. For this purpose, represented in Figure 
3.5, using the industry standard pvlib Python library to model photovoltaic system 
performance, the real energy output to be produced was mathematically reconstructed for the 
historical conditions. Simulation was set to match a typical residential setting in Sarawak.  
 
A virtual photovoltaic system was created with a total capacity of 5-kilowatt peak (kWp) 
in the form of standard monocrystalline silicon modules. Due to local constraints of 
architecture, this configuration was built based on an inclination of 5 degrees slanting to South 
(Azimuth 180°) with the position of the flat roof terrace in accordance with the flat roof terrace 
layout. The simulation also considered the thermal degradation effect, and a temperature 
coefficient of negative 0.4% per degree Celsius was applied, indicating that during periods of 
high atmospheric temperature, performance is lowered dynamically. Feeding the preprocessed 
meteorological inputs irradiance and temperature into this physics engine, this system 
calculates electrical power results for each hour the two years of data are contained. This 
approach finally led to the creation of a new data column, Target_Solar_Power_kW, which 
stands as the final Target Variable (Y) for training the machine learning algorithm.

3.3.4. Exploratory Data Analysis Protocol 
When using the synthesized dataset for training the model, we conduct an extensive

3.4.1. Concept Definition 
To quantify the specific improvements offered by the proposed AI optimization, a Baseline

the comparative review of existing systems conducted in Chapter 2, the "Self-Consumption"

Consistent with the limitations identified in Chapter 2, the fundamental weakness of this

present-time data, the controller fails to optimize for Time-of-Use (ToU) tariff variances (Pan

As we can see in the figure 3.9, the baseline system discharges the battery continuously in 
the early morning time window (00:00 to 06:00) to compensate for non-essential night loads. 
Because the controller is not predictive, it depletes the storage down to its minimum safety

3.5. Proposed Method: AI-Powered Optimization 
3.5.1. Development of Forecasting Module 
A twenty-four-hour solar power generation forecasting module (in the form of a machine 
learning module) controls the central intelligence of the proposed system. The algorithm used 
for this regression task is based upon the Random Forest Regressor framework, which is an 
ensemble learning model built from multiple decision trees. In line with the previous 
comparative study carried out in Chapter 2, this architecture was chosen over deep learning 
approaches (e.g., LSTM) with respect to stability on mid-sized datasets (~17,000 observations) 
and as it also captures non-linear meteorological relationships without the potential hazards of 
the neural network overfitting (Voyant et al., 2017).

capture the physical drivers of photovoltaic generation and therefore establish a robust mapping 
between the environmental conditions and the energy yield through model training. The Input

relationship with solar irradiance. The most important predictor is Global Horizontal Irradiance

However, as far as the forecast performance is concerned, this dataset undergoes an

random shuffling that ignores temporal dependencies, the dataset is then split up using a strict

3.5.2. Development of Optimization Algorithm 
In contrast to the reactive baseline presented here, the proposed optimization algorithm 
presents a predictive layer, drastically changing the battery dispatch strategy. The key 
improvement is the integration of the forecast data in the decision matrix that enables the 
controller to analyze the energy available range over a rolling twenty-four-hour horizon. There 
is no one-size-fits-all scenario here, but the application runs intelligent logic directly targeted 
to Sarawak Energy Tariff C3 structure with Peak charges set to be high between 7:00 AM and 
10:00 PM (Sarawak Energy, n.d.). The core of the control innovation with the new feature is 
the establishment of the conditional "Conservation Mode". 
 
This mode is applied when the forecasting module expects the sum of solar generation of 
the next day to drop below a certain threshold, which could be classified as heavy overcast or 
rainy. For example, under usual baseline logic, the battery would be discharged to run empty 
at night for minor loads. But this behavior is overridden by the proposed "Smart" rule. When a 
low-solar event is anticipated, the controller limits nighttime discharging to save the stored 
energy. This guarantees that the battery remains sufficient State of Charge to discharge during 
the high-cost morning peak hours of the day to replace costly grid imports with stored 
renewable energy in the event that solar generation is not possible. The proactive energy 
resource shifting is the primary mechanism of the expected economic efficiency improvements.
