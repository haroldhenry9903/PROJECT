ABSTRACT
Residential photovoltaic (PV) adoption in Sarawak is limited by solar intermittency and a mismatch between midday generation and evening demand. Under Time of Use (ToU) pricing, which is already applied commercially and plausible for future residential tariffs, reactive self consumption can discharge stored energy during cheap off peak periods and leave insufficient reserves for expensive peak hours. This project develops an AI assisted, rule based battery control strategy evaluated through a Python digital twin simulation of a grid tied home with a 5 kWp PV array and a 10 kWh battery. Historical meteorological data from five Sarawak divisions (Kuching, Miri, Bintulu, Kapit, and Sri Aman) drive physics based solar output modelling and day ahead hourly solar power forecasts using Random Forest and Support Vector Regression. Forecasts activate a proactive Conservation Mode on predicted low solar days, compared against a conventional self consumption baseline under a simplified peak and off peak tariff. Results show consistent annual electricity cost reductions across all regions (approximately RM 9 to 26 per simulated year, depending on location and forecaster), with statistically significant daily savings. An interactive Streamlit dashboard presents forecasts, energy flows, and economic outcomes. The study demonstrates that forecast informed, interpretable control can improve residential economics under tropical ToU conditions without physical hardware deployment.
Keywords:	Residential Photovoltaics, Battery Control Strategy, Solar Power Forecasting, Time of Use Tariff, Digital Twin Simulation

INTRODUCTION
Study Background
Global efforts to combat climate change and improve energy security have accelerated the adoption of renewable technologies, with solar power emerging as a key solution due to its vast natural abundance (Ahmed et al., 2025). Solar photovoltaic (PV) power is currently the most widely used electricity source globally based on the number of installations due to the ongoing reduction in PV panel prices and the rise in solar cell efficiency (Ahmed et al., 2025).
Even though solar PV systems are widely used and reasonably priced, their energy generation during daylight is exceedingly unpredictable as it is closely related to changing weather conditions (Soo et al., 2025). This variability, combined with the sun's daily cycle, causes a mismatch between supply and demand. Solar power is abundant at midday, but households use the most energy when the sun is low, especially in the mornings and evenings. Moreover, this mismatch becomes a major financial burden under Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs, a pricing system where electricity costs significantly more during specific times of the day. Sarawak Energy Berhad currently implements ToU tariffs for the commercial and industrial sectors, such as Tariff C3, making the extension of this pricing structure to residential users a logical and technically feasible step toward demand-side management.
While initiatives like the Sarawak Alternative Rural Electrification Scheme (SARES) operate off-grid to ensure basic reliability, applying this same technology to a grid-tied residence fundamentally shifts the objective toward economic optimization (Memon et al., 2023). We need more than hardware that reacts to current conditions; we need a smart system that proactively anticipates future requirements (Mohammad & Mahjabeen, 2023).
  This Final Year Project develops a reproducible Python simulation platform, a digital twin for a grid-tied residential PV and battery system under a configurable peak/off-peak ToU tariff (Muzmar et al., 2015). Unlike a single-city case study, the implemented system evaluates five administrative divisions in Sarawak: Kuching, Miri, Bintulu, Kapit, and Sri Aman, using region-specific latitude and longitude when synthesizing PV output. Historical weather fields are prepared as strict hourly time series, gap-filled and split per region, then converted into AC-side PV power using a physics-based pvlib ModelChain, producing a ground-truth column (Target_Power_kW) used consistently by forecasting, baseline simulation, and smart control.
Day-ahead decisions are supported by machine learning regressors that predict hourly Target_Power_kW from seven inputs: global horizontal irradiance, temperature, humidity, precipitation, cloud cover, hour, and month. To study algorithmic robustness, the project trains and compares two scikit-learn estimators per region: a Random Forest regressor and a Support Vector Regression (RBF kernel) inside a pipeline with feature scaling. Both models share the same chronological split and time-series cross-validation so differences in savings reflect modeling bias, not a different controller implementation.
The smart controller is not a black-box end-to-end reinforcement learner. It is a transparent, forecast-informed rule-based policy whose parameters include a low-solar energy threshold and a state-of-charge reserve for peak-period discharge prioritization. Each midnight, the policy estimates the next-day total PV energy from the 24-hour ahead hourly predictions and activates "Conservation Mode" on predicted low-solar days. Because the PV target is a deterministic function of weather inputs, the implementation deliberately applies a seeded forecast-error perturbation to the next-day weather features before prediction, preventing an unrealistic perfect-foresight oracle that would inflate savings. A multi-seed sensitivity sweep over forecast noise intensity is used to report the mean and variability of annual savings.
The work is packaged as a configuration-driven pipeline (experiment_config.json) orchestrated by run_all.py, which exports per-region, per-algorithm result bundles. A Streamlit dashboard provides an interactive interface to load a saved run, switch regions and forecasting algorithms, and inspect training diagnostics, validation checks, statistical significance tests, and sensitivity curves.

Problem Statement 
The fundamental technical challenge hindering the efficiency of grid-tied hybrid solar-battery systems in Sarawak is the stochastic nature of solar irradiance combined with the lack of predictive control logic in typical residential energy management (Mohammad & Mahjabeen, 2023). In tropical climates like Sarawak, high-frequency cloud cover and seasonal monsoon variations create severe fluctuations in power generation known as intermittency (Soo et al., 2025). This creates a temporal mismatch between peak generation during midday and peak residential demand in the evening, which becomes a critical financial liability under Time-of-Use (ToU) tariff structures where electricity rates surge during specific peak windows for example, Sarawak Energy Tariff C3 in the commercial sector, which motivates analogous residential pricing in future grid-modernisation scenarios (Muzmar et al., 2015).   
Current commercial approaches often rely on deterministic reactive control or self-consumption logic (Pan et al., 2022). These systems follow strict, largely static rules: they charge when solar exceeds load and discharge when load exceeds solar. The shortcoming of such an approach is that it is myopic: it responds only to the present situation without adequate consideration of the near future (Notton et al., 2018). For example, a reactive controller may discharge the battery during a cheap off-peak period to support a small load, thereby depleting reserves needed for an expensive peak window later the same day (Pan et al., 2022). Moreover, traditional timer-based scheduling is insufficient because it cannot adapt to variable daily weather; a fixed discharge plan may fail if the battery never charged due to an unexpected afternoon storm (Notton et al., 2018).   
This lack of look-ahead capability leads to suboptimal energy dispatch, where stored renewable energy is used during low-value periods, forcing the homeowner to import expensive grid power during peak hours.  At the same time, Sarawak’s geography produces different weather regimes across divisions; a solution validated in only one city may not generalise (Soo et al., 2025). There remains a need for research that integrates Sarawak-relevant solar forecasting with interpretable, rule-based battery control under ToU economics, and that quantifies whether such forecasting can outperform standard non-predictive controllers in a simulation environment that is rigorous enough for academic evaluation. This project addresses that gap through a reproducible digital-twin study; the detailed methodology and metrics are defined in Chapter 3.
Objectives
The objectives of this project are:
To develop a multi-region digital twin simulation framework for a grid-tied PV and battery system, integrating machine learning forecasting (Random Forest and SVR) with a predictive rule-based controller to optimize energy dispatch under Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs.
To evaluate the system’s economic and operational performance against deterministic baselines using rigorous statistical and sensitivity analyses, and to deliver an interactive Streamlit dashboard for reproducible visualization of the results.

Scope
The scope of this project is strictly limited to the design, development, and simulation of an optimization system within a Python environment. The study models a hybrid solar battery system connected to the grid using several key parameters. First, it uses a representative residential configuration, such as a rooftop solar array with 10 kilowatt hours of battery storage and inverter power limits appropriate to a domestic setting. Second, it utilizes predefined synthetic residential load profiles suitable for controlled comparison rather than data from smart meters in real homes. Third, it incorporates historical meteorological data for multiple Sarawak regions, including Kuching, Miri, Bintulu, Kapit, and Sri Aman, obtained and prepared for simulation purposes. Finally, it employs solar generation modeling utilizing the library to simulate physical characteristics.
The policy targets cost reduction under an assumed future residential ToU tariff (peak/off-peak), which is a reasonable scenario given existing commercial ToU structures and demand-side management trends (Muzmar et al., 2015). An interactive Streamlit dashboard showcases system behaviour in this simulated environment. Explicitly excluded from scope are real-time control of physical hardware, live utility or inverter integration, home load forecasting, feed-in tariff revenue modelling, distribution-network constraints, and advanced optimisation paradigms such as reinforcement learning as the primary controller are excluded to keep the project feasible and interpretable within the FYP timeframe.  Detailed evaluation protocols and sensitivity studies are described in Chapter 3, not in this introductory chapter.

Significance of Project
This study is important because it provides a strategic approach to increasing the economic viability of hybrid solar battery systems among Sarawak households. In an environment already operational in the commercial sector and anticipated for residential expansion, the project moves beyond thinking constrained by fixed pricing and examines when energy is imported from the grid rather than only how much (Muzmar et al., 2015). It supports a quantifiable narrative: shifting battery use away from expensive peak periods by using forecast-informed control rather than reactive self-consumption alone.
By covering multiple Sarawak regions, the work strengthens the claim that the proposed approach is relevant across different local weather conditions, not only in one divisional capital. The AI-powered method also contributes to the broader technical discussion of demand-side management in tropical climates showing, in simulation, that residential systems can anticipate solar availability and reduce grid reliance during critical demand hours. This aligns individual homeowner savings with utility goals to ease peak load pressure and offers an intelligible basis for energy management under Sarawak's economic and climatic context.

Literature review
Introduction
The shift to renewables in tropical climates poses a specific engineering paradox due to the solar potential that is present in those regions, but whose intermittency poses significant challenges for stable and economical grid operation. This chapter reviews theoretical and technological foundations with which to resolve this tension in Sarawak. The review is designed to let the reader follow the logical journey of the system design. The discussion starts by studying the base mechanics behind photovoltaic conversion and then considers the stochastic nature of solar intermittency and the need for hybrid battery storage solutions. Following are sections to assess the economic conditions that are moving from mere off grid reliability to complicated grid-tied cost optimization under Time of Use tariffs. To overcome these problems, the review provides a critical analysis of modern forecasting based on a specific choice of Machine Learning algorithms that should justify the use of them over old statistical algorithms. Finally, the chapter compares the state-of-the-art control methods, from deterministic rule-based logic to advanced reinforcement learning that can be applied and indicates the research gaps that this project will try to fill by offering a Digital Twin simulation framework. Specifically, this framework simulates a grid-tied residential system across five distinct Sarawak regions, prioritizing Time-of-Use cost optimization through an import-only accounting model. Furthermore, to ensure realistic day-ahead dispatch without relying on perfect oracle weather data, the simulation introduces an explicit forecast-error model and evaluates both Random Forest and Support Vector Regression (SVR) algorithms.
Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Systems and Intermittency

Photovoltaic (PV) power generation relies on the photovoltaic effect, a phenomenon first discovered by Becquerel in 1839, which enables the direct conversion of sunlight into electricity (Singh, 2013). This conversion is realized via the photovoltaic cell, a semiconductor unit that converts solar light into electrical energy via a solid-state process. These conversion phenomena are mathematically modeled using an equivalent circuit that encodes the electrical characteristics of the solar cell. The Challenge of Stochastic Intermittency
The inherent stochastic and intermittent nature of solar energy presents a significant challenge for electrical grid integration because photovoltaic (PV) generation fluctuates randomly across time scales ranging from seconds to seasons (Notton et al., 2018). Unlike conventional power sources, solar output is heavily dependent on variable environmental factors like cloud cover and daily cycles, making it difficult for grid operators to maintain the mandatory real-time balance between electricity production and consumption. This unpredictability forces utility managers to maintain expensive energy reserves and keep backup generators running at inefficient levels to immediately compensate for sudden drops in solar power. Consequently, this requirement for additional flexibility and specific reserves to handle unexpected ramp rates significantly increases the operational costs and technical complexity of integrating high levels of PV penetration into the standard power grid.

Hybrid Solar Battery Systems as a 	Solution
System Architecture and Functionality
A hybrid renewable energy system solves the stochastic intermittency of solar power by providing a complete solution by combining the generation of solar power through photovoltaic technology with battery energy storage and utility grid connections to ensure uninterrupted electric power supplies to end users. As a result, this architecture is based on the battery energy storage system, which absorbs solar energy in high solar irradiation periods and releases electric energy when generation has dropped or demand has increased (León Gómez et al., 2023). Having a connected hybrid network for system configuration enables effective management of the power flow between direct current solar panels and battery on the one hand and alternating current utility grid on the other hand. This is a combination which not only stabilizes the voltage and frequency, on top of that makes electricity supply more efficient and reliable compared to monolithic electricity systems.
To materialize this tactic, an architecture consists of the 4 major parts of the architecture: PV panels, battery storage unit, DC to AC inverter, and utility connection represented in Figure 2.2. In this topology, this photovoltaic PV system generates direct current electricity which goes directly into either the storage reservoir or directly into the inverter (Gabr et al., 2021). Application in Sarawak: Off-Grid Reliability
In Sarawak, rural electrification has been achieved with standalone solar power systems under the Sarawak Alternative Rural Electrification Scheme (SARES) by providing reliable power to remote villages where extending the main utility grid is economically unfeasible (Chong et al., 2020). Unlike the hybrid renewable energy system which can tap into the national utility to manage the supply and demand, these self-sustained designs have no dependence on the national grid to supply electricity. As such, although such standalone systems have already established good reliability with an interruption frequency lower than the general distribution network, they don't have the backup security of the grid, which means they are compelled to adopt strict energy quotas, such as a daily limit of 2 kWh per household, to avoid depletion of batteries and ensure long term sustainability. In contrast to these rural standalone models, this thesis focuses on grid-connected residential users in urban and suburban environments who can import power during deficits and are exposed to time-differentiated energy prices. Therefore, the primary optimization objective of this research is bill minimization and peak-import reduction under grid availability, rather than absolute standalone autonomy.

Economic Drivers: From Reliability to Optimization
The Paradigm Shifts: Grid-Tied Economic Optimization
With the urbanization of Sarawak, adaptation of proven hybrid solar technology for grid connected residential applications is crucial. The main operational target in this scenario will not be energy sufficiency but rather economic optimization. Standalone systems such as SARES focus on continuous power supply whereas urban grid connected systems must grapple with financial constraints by avoiding expensive electricity imports during peak pricing hours. This shift requires a change from backup strategies to smart dispatch algorithms optimized to minimize costs.
The Evolution of Pricing Mechanisms (Tariff Structures)
The power tariff’s structure, which dictates the cost of energy consumption at different times of day, plays a key role in the economic optimization of grid-connected systems. As a model, Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs introduce a dynamic pricing model to electricity grid-connected systems, with prices that vary at different times of day according to usage patterns and demand. When compared to the traditional flat rate tariff system, the former uses a dynamic pricing model under which the price of electricity in a power grid reflects these varying charges. Using a ToU system, the day is segmented into periods when prices are significantly higher during peak hours, as the demand for electricity is greatest, and substantially lower during off-peak hours when consumption drops (Pan et al., 2022). This dynamic mechanism generates effective price signals to encourage consumers to shift usage away from expensive periods and transforms the battery from a passive backup to an active financial instrument capable of executing energy arbitrage by storing low-cost energy and discharging it during high-cost windows.
Local Context and the Optimization Challenge
Within the Malaysian context, advanced pricing mechanisms can be seen by reviewing the composition of domestic tariffs to establish a Time-of-Use (ToU) scheme designed to raise energy efficiency by creating different costs. This framework, available to homes with smart meters, sharply isolates electric bills, increasing off-peak hours to cover all hours of the day on weekends and from 10:00 PM to 2:00 PM the next day on weekdays (Ramli, 2025).
While Tenaga Nasional Berhad (TNB) attempts to impose Time-of-Use schemes on the peninsula, Sarawak Energy Berhad adopts a unique framework with its C3 tariff system currently applicable to commercial consumers. Nonetheless, this pricing scheme serves as an ongoing barometer for potential future dynamic pricing in the residential market. As illustrated in Figure 2.4, under this way, the tariff charges a premium Peak Period rate of 24.5 sen/kWh and a RM 20.00 Maximum Demand charge per kilowatt. The Off-Peak window of 13.9 sen/kWh offers a further discount on the tariff (Sarawak Energy, n.d.). Therefore, the C3 tariff structure serves strictly as policy context for this research; the simulation utilizes the peak rate of 0.245 RM/kWh and the off-peak rate of 0.139 RM/kWh to conduct a what-if residential ToU study, rather than attempting to construct a full commercial bill replica. In this scenario, an imprecise controller may accidentally discharge early and deplete the battery before peak pricing starts. As a result, this is a complex stochastic optimization problem with no easy answers and control logic must be more complex if it is not limited to simple rule-based operation to be capable of predicting future solar output and load situations with high speed forecasting so as not only to make a strategic energy dispatch allocation but also aggressively harvest the off-peak time in order to efficiently charge and discharge the battery before peak demand charges are achieved by harvesting as few times as possible.

Machine Learning for Solar Forecasting
The Shift to Data-Driven Forecasting
To transition solar power forecasting from an underlying theory of solar generation to an actual forecast driven by data-driven methods, these advanced algorithms will replace the primitive and static models, paving the way for the generation of new solutions. According to Long et al. (2014), these computational methods for analysis of real-time solar radiation are better than those that use the traditional weather-based techniques as they make use of the already established weather data, namely temperature and insolation, which are found most critical, resulting in forecasts at much lower error rates. In contrast to the previous persistent approach which merely assumes that the predicted future solar output will be equal to the current state, the data-driven techniques utilize the patterns in the training data for learning to minimize the Root Mean Square Error and serve as a more stable grid management solution. So, such algorithms as the Artificial Neural Network can produce the most accurate prediction for immediate daily forecast, confirming the effectiveness of incorporating ML in renewables planning.
Overview of Prominent Algorithms
Given the existing literature on computational architectures in renewable energy, to find the best prediction engine for the simulation, the proposed computational architecture needs to be reviewed. A wide number of references from published literature show that we have three specific machine learning algorithms that demonstrate better prediction of the non-linear stochastic nature of solar irradiance. The successful approaches are Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Random Forest Regressors (RF) for a particular application in tropical weather. The technical analysis of these algorithms, based on their applicability and suitability, is followed by a section of this paper where it will discuss their suitability for the specific constraints of the Sarawak environment. Rather than selecting a single algorithm purely based on this literature, the implemented pipeline treats both Random Forest and Support Vector Regression as comparable supervised regressors. Both models are trained using the exact same seven meteorological input features, enabling a direct empirical comparison of their performance rather than relying on a predetermined conclusion.
Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)
For accurate prediction of solar radiation, Artificial Neural Networks, and the multilayer perceptron model using feed-forward back-propagation as its underlying theory, can be an invaluable tool to model the intricate, non-linear relationships (Notton et al., 2019). As evident from Figure 2.5, the core architecture has been constructed in parallel such that the input layer receives meteorological factors, the hidden layer processes the information with synaptic weights, and the output layer supplies the predicted solar irradiation figures. Each neuron operates in this system by computing a weighted sum of inputs while applying the corresponding activation function (sigmoid for the hidden layer and linear for the output layer) to find the level of signal passing to the next level. To find the best tradeoff between its performance and computational cost in the optimization of accuracy, the network design often is composed of having only one hidden layer and it takes advantage of training algorithms such as Levenberg-Marquardt in order to constantly modify the connection weights to obtain the lowest prediction error.

Figure 2.5: Architecture of an artificial neuron and a multi-layered neural network (Notton et al., 2019)

Support Vector Regression (SVR)
Support Vector Regression further extends the classification prowess of Support Vector Machines to the regression domain, providing a strong framework for solar power forecasting in nonlinear weather scenarios utilizing the kernel trick (Abuella & Chowdhury, 2017). Unlike traditional regression methods that minimize the error between the predicted and actual values for all data points, in SVR there is a designated insensitive region around the regression function where errors falling within this region are ignored if they fall within a defined threshold . As depicted in Figure 2.6, this architecture constructs a boundary margin that envelops the regression line; only data points falling outside this "tube" serve as the input to the cost function, which is minimized by balancing the model's complexity against these deviations. As a result of mapping input vectors into a higher dimensional feature space, SVR converts nonlinear relationships into linear ones, thereby providing a global and unique solution to overcome overfitting problems faced in commonly used neural networks.

Figure 2.6: The Boundary margin and loss setting for a linear SVR (Abuella & Chowdhury, 2017)

Random Forest Regressor (RFR)
Random Forest Regression is built on the robust ensemble of learning architecture of Bootstrap Aggregating, which allows performance to be increased with more decision trees built in the training stage (Babu et al., 2025). The model achieves this as shown in Figure 2.7 by building multiple bootstrapped subsets of the original dataset and training a separate decision tree on each subset at the same time rather than through sequential training. In this structure each tree splits the data independently by optimizing the mean square error to minimize variance and capture complex data patterns. A final forecast output is obtained from the average of all individual predictions, thus effectively reducing the overfitting risk often associated with individual tree models, as well as ensuring high accuracy for solar generation and demand forecasting.

Overview of Control Paradigms
To achieve this optimization, two primary control paradigms are prevalent in literature:
Reinforcement Learning (RL)
Reinforcement Learning refers to a data-driven optimization approach: an intelligent agent learns to make decisions through continuous interaction with a dynamic environment (Liu & Gou, 2025). As seen here in the Figure 2.8 Reinforcement Learning schematic, the architecture becomes a closed loop involving state observation, action execution, and reward calculation. In the specific context of battery optimization, the agent watches the current state of a system (), typically consisting of multidimensional variables such as real time photovoltaic generation, household load demand, and battery’s State of Charge. To do so, the agent creates a controlled action (specific action space) to have the battery charge, discharge or remain idle for some period. After completing this action, the environment gives feedback in terms of a numerical reward (), reflecting how well the decision was executed in decreasing its dependence on the grid or costs. During successive iteration, the agent constantly updates its policy network to maximize the sum reward, thereby learning the best (and most efficient) energy dispatch control strategy without relying on a fixed, pre-defined mathematical model of the physics of the building.

Figure 2.8: Architecture of RL (Liu & Gou, 2025)

Rule Based Control (RBC)
Rule Based Control serves as a deterministic optimization strategy in which predetermined logical sequences guide control of energy flow (Liu & Gou, 2025). Architecture follows a hierarchical decision-making structure, as illustrated with Rule Based Control logic flowchart in Figure 2.9. It starts with an Input layer, where real-time system variables such as photovoltaic generation and load demand are ingested. This data then flows through to a central conditional node, represented by the diamond symbol, which compares the current state with specific thresholds or Boolean conditions. Based on this appraisal, the logic splits into separate paths where certain Calculations must be performed to determine the exact power requirements, for example, how much surplus solar energy there is to be had. Following this quantitative step, a Process phase is taken. Then the control command is formalized for finalization, and the actions it carries out, such as charging the battery or transferring power to the grid, happen. That sequential "If Then" system ensures that operation of the battery is strictly aligned with established priority rules at each step (without necessity for further learning).

Comparative Analysis
Selecting the appropriate control architecture requires a holistic evaluation that moves beyond theoretical potential to examine empirical operational performance. To determine the optimal strategy for a residential energy management system, it is essential to benchmark the candidates against tangible engineering constraints including energy efficiency, grid stability, and computational transparency. The following analysis synthesizes experimental data from Liu and Gou (2025), who conducted a rigorous comparative study between a stochastic Reinforcement Learning model and a deterministic Rule Based Controller under identical environmental conditions. This evaluation separates the performance outcomes into quantitative economic metrics and qualitative operational assessments to fully justify the algorithmic selection.
Quantitative Performance Metrics
To rigorously evaluate the economic efficiency of the control strategies, Liu and Gou (2025) conducted a comparative study using real world residential load data. The primary metric for assessment was the Energy Self Sufficiency Rate (SSR), which measures the percentage of household demand met by onsite solar and storage. The empirical results demonstrated that the Rule Based Control strategy achieved a superior Self Sufficiency Rate of 94%, whereas the Reinforcement Learning model using Proximal Policy Optimization reached a lower peak of 86%. This performance gap highlights that the deterministic logic of the Rule Based Control is more effective at maximizing the utilization of stored energy. Furthermore, regarding grid dependency, the study highlighted that in specific test scenarios (Case 1), the Rule Based Control successfully eliminated grid reliance entirely by reducing grid imports to 0.00 kWh. In contrast, the Reinforcement Learning agent still required 4.21 kWh of imported electricity to meet the same load demand, indicating a lower efficiency in mitigating costs during critical periods.
Qualitative Operational Metrics
And, beyond sheer numerical efficiency, the control strategy stability operationally is crucial for battery health as well as grid compliance (Liu and Gou, 2025). The study found a significant qualitative difference in the power dispatch profiles produced for the two models. It is in this case that the Reinforcement Learning model has clearly observed distinct sawtooth variations between charging and discharging profiles, due to the stochastic randomness inherent in its strategy exploration process. These abrupt and unpredictable switching processes can cause fluctuations in local grid voltages, leading to fast physical deterioration of battery cells. In contrast, the Rule Based Control maintained a smoother and more continuous operation profile, ensuring that the battery discharge frequency is consistent with the peak loads without irrelevant perturbations. In addition, when it comes to system clarity, Reinforcement Learning performs as a black box model with unfathomable decision logic for the system operator. Alternatively, Rule Based Control provides an interpretable and logical paradigm which enables engineers to check the nature of their decision on when to dispatch equipment and verify that compliance with safety constraints is rigorously maintained.

Comparative Review of Existing Systems
Overview of Current Approaches
To position the Sarawak specific simulation in the broader academic context, it is important to consider how recent studies have addressed the challenges of solar forecasting, battery optimization, and system sizing. The current literature tends to isolate these problems, focusing either on off grid reliability, heavy computational sizing, or complex deep learning models. However, looking at four notable works presented in the last few years, it is clear the research gaps in this work we propose to address.
System A: Off Grid Reliability vs. Grid Tied Economics
In the autonomous energy systems domain, Babu et al. (2025) provided a comprehensive framework titled "Forecasting and optimization of a residential off-grid solar photovoltaic-battery energy storage system." They specialize in the context of Bangladesh, where the aim is to realize full energy independence from the national utility grid. To do this, they have proposed a data-driven architectural framework (Figure 2.10), providing a pathway to a sequential pipeline starting from data acquisition to machine learning-based forecasting using algorithms (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting), and finally system sizing optimization. Their basic operational goal is reliability, specifically ensuring that the solar PV and battery capacities are sufficiently large to sustain load continuity without any external grid support.

Figure 2.10: Methodology of machine learning modelling (Babu et al., 2025)

However, a critical divergence exists between their approach and the requirements of an urban residential setting in Sarawak. The framework proposed by Babu et al. (2025) operates under a paradigm of Energy Constraints, where the system must be oversized to guarantee survival during low generation periods. For instance, their optimization logic resulted in scaling the photovoltaic capacity from 11 kW to 30 kW and deploying a massive 120 kWh battery to ensure autonomy. This approach effectively treats the battery as a static backup reservoir rather than an active financial asset.
In contrast, the proposed Sarawak simulation operates under a paradigm of Financial Constraints defined by the Time of Use Tariff C3 structure. Unlike the off-grid model which ignores electricity prices to focus on availability, this project assumes the grid is always available but financially penalizing during peak hours. Therefore, the research gap identified here is the transition from Passive Reliability Sizing to Active Economic Dispatch. While Babu et al. utilize Random Forest to determine how big the system must be to avoid blackout, this project utilizes Random Forest to determine when a fixed size system should discharge to minimize costs. This distinction highlights the necessity for a control logic that prioritizes price arbitrage over simple state of charge preservation, addressing the specific economic needs of grid tied prosumers that are not covered by off grid reliability models. Thus, while similarly employing tree-based regression for forecasting, the present work applies it across five distinct Sarawak weather trajectories to optimize daily dispatch timing under ToU tariffs, rather than calculating kW/kWh hardware expansion to maintain standalone autonomy.

Their research specifically targets computational bottlenecks inherent in traditional optimization methods like Linear Programming (LP). To solve this, they introduced a hierarchical control architecture where a central entity provides market signals to a local Battery Management System (BMS). The core of their innovation is a three-step deterministic algorithm, as illustrated in the flowcharts of their methodology (Figures 2.11, 2.12, and 2.13), which sequentially calculates the energy availability, determines the target energy exchange, and finally schedules the specific battery charging or discharging intervals. This "Rule Based" approach was proven to be approximately 30 times faster than equivalent linear programming solvers while maintaining similar economic performance.

Yet a profound limitation in their study is a major research gap regarding its application. This framework by Becchi et al. As an output, the system transforms from a dry scheduling tool to a practical operational controller that can account for the stochastic nature of the Sarawak climate. This project makes sure that such speed-based decision logic is based on realistic, probabilistic weather data, rather than idealized assumptions, by leveraging the computational ease of rule-based control and evaluating how these predictive errors impact downstream cost savings, successfully separating near-perfect theoretical behaviour from noisy day-ahead reality.

By assuming a fixed hardware capacity, the proposed "Digital Twin" simulation focuses entirely on the Operational Intelligence required to maximize daily arbitrage. This shift from static capacity planning to dynamic, forecast driven control ensures that residential prosumers can actively minimize their monthly bills using the hardware they already own, a capability not covered by commercial sizing algorithms. Furthermore, by executing this dynamic residential simulation across five distinct meteorological regions in Sarawak, this research provides a multi-region operational validation that specifically contrasts with their localized commercial scope.

methodology
Introduction
This chapter describes the technical methodology used to build and evaluate an AI-assisted battery control policy for a grid-tied residential PV and storage model, implemented as a deterministic hourly simulation in Python. The study adopts a comparative experimental design: a reactive baseline controller is compared against a forecast-informed smart controller under identical per-hour synthetic load, identical battery physics, and identical time-of-use (ToU) tariff parameters, while photovoltaic generation varies with historical weather reconstructed separately for five Sarawak regions (Kuching, Miri, Bintulu, Kapit, Sri Aman).
While the overall project management may follow an Agile Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) to structure development milestones, the scientific method applied here is a quantitative simulation with fixed seeds and a single machine-readable configuration (experiment_config.json). This ensures that every numerical assumption—coordinates, model grids, battery size, tariff rates, controller thresholds, forecast-error models, and sweep ranges—is strictly auditable and reproducible.
The central hypothesis is that predictive lookahead from an AI forecaster enables a day-ahead operating mode ("Conservation Mode") that reduces grid electricity costs compared with myopic self-consumption, without altering the underlying electrochemical hardware. This methodology extends beyond a single-site prototype to test whether conclusions are regionally stable across Sarawak, and whether performance is dependent on choosing a Random Forest versus a Support Vector Regression (SVR with RBF kernel) forecasting model.

General Research Framework
System Architecture
The system functions as a "Digital Twin" and is implemented via two distinct architectural layers:

Figure 3.1: System Architecture Block Diagram

Offline Preparation and Learning
This layer handles data ingestion, cleaning, and model training. Multi-site hourly weather data is ingested and processed, and AC PV power (Target_Power_kW) is synthesized via pvlib using the specific latitude and longitude of each site. The layer then trains and selects two alternative regressors per region (Random Forest and a StandardScaler + SVR pipeline). These algorithms map a seven-dimensional weather and calendar feature vector to hourly PV power.
Online Simulation
This layer replays the full timeline hour-by-hour using a shared Battery state-space model (core_battery_model.py). The baseline policy reacts to the instantaneous net load without foresight. The smart policy (phase4_smart_optimizer.py) queries the trained model each midnight for a 24-hour feature window. It compares the predicted daily PV energy to a configurable threshold () and toggles a Conservation_Mode_Active flag for that day. On conservation days, off-peak discharge power is restricted to hold charge for the impending peak window, restoring full discharge capabilities once peak rates begin.
Hardware and Economic Constants
The reference configuration utilizes a nominal 5 kWp DC nameplate in the pvlib system definition (surface tilt 5°, azimuth 180°, linear temperature coefficient -0.004 / °C). The battery is configured with 10 kWh of usable energy, a 5 kW AC-limited charge/discharge cap, a minimum State of Charge (SoC) of 0.15, and a 0.95 round-trip efficiency (implemented as  per direction). Under the configured Sarawak ToU tariff, peak hours are 07:00 to 22:00 at 0.245 RM/kWh, with off-peak hours at 0.139 RM/kWh.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing
Meteorological Data Sourcing
Historical weather telemetry for this study was originally sourced and downloaded via the Visual Crossing Weather Data Services API. This raw data is provided as a multi-site hourly dataset (swkdataweather.csv) bundled within the project repository for total reproducibility. The file aggregates data for five distinct Sarawak regions. Each site contributes 43,872 hourly records spanning from 2020-01-01 to 2025-01-01 23:00, yielding 219,360 raw rows total. The radiative data includes global horizontal irradiance (GHI), direct normal irradiance (DNI), and diffuse horizontal irradiance (DHI), alongside ambient temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, and cloud cover.
Data Preprocessing and Cleaning
Preprocessing ensures strict hourly alignment per region. After timezone localization to Asia/Kuching (UTC+8), missing values are addressed using a combination of linear interpolation (capped at three consecutive gaps) and forward/backward filling. Radiative columns are clamped at zero to remove any sensor noise causing negative outputs. The output files form the direct inputs for the physical synthesis engine.
Synthetic Generation of Ground Truth
Because historical generation logs from a physical installation were unavailable, ground-truth PV power is mathematically synthesized using pvlib.modelchain.ModelChain with the physical AOI model. This generates an AC power profile (Target_Power_kW), which serves as the unified truth consumed by both the baseline/smart simulations and the machine learning regressors in Phase 3.
Exploratory Data Analysis Protocol
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is generated per region (or as 5-panel figures) to examine multicollinearity among the seven final predictors and the target PV power. The analysis confirms the expected strong positive correlations between solar irradiance parameters and generation, alongside the inverse relationship of cloud opacity during the Northeast Monsoon.
Baseline Method Formulation
Concept Definition
To quantify the AI's value, a conventional "Self-Consumption" algorithm is established as the baseline. This deterministic controller is completely reactive, meaning it buffers excess solar or discharges to support loads based strictly on the current hour's state, totally lacking forward-looking predictive intelligence.
Control Logic
The baseline calculates the instantaneous net power mismatch between load and solar generation. If there is a surplus, the battery charges up to its 5 kW AC limit. If there is a deficit, the battery discharges to cover the home load up to the 5 kW limit, provided it remains above the 0.15 SoC floor. Any residual deficit is covered by grid imports. The physical limits are governed strictly by the battery's state-space equation incorporating the round-trip efficiency penalty.
Limitations
The primary limitation of this myopic approach is its failure to account for Time-of-Use tariffs. The controller naturally discharges early in the morning (e.g., 00:00 to 06:00) to cover trivial loads. By the time the expensive peak window starts at 07:00 (charged at 0.245 RM/kWh compared to the 0.139 RM/kWh off-peak rate), the battery is frequently depleted, forcing the household to purchase expensive peak-time electricity.
Proposed Method: AI-Powered Optimization
To overcome the myopic limitations of the standard baseline controller, this research introduces an AI-powered optimization framework. This proposed method shifts the operational paradigm from reactive load-matching to proactive energy management. By integrating a machine learning forecasting module with a deterministic rule-based controller, the system is designed to predict future photovoltaic generation and execute strategic energy arbitrage under the Time-of-Use tariff structure. The following subsections detail the architectural development of the predictive models, including feature engineering and rigorous validation protocols, followed by the formulation of the smart conservation logic that governs the daily battery dispatch.

System Architecture and Repository Layout
The software framework is designed around a modular, decoupled sequential architecture where data preparation, physical modeling, machine learning training, and system simulations are strictly separated into independent pipeline phases. This architectural choice ensures that the digital twin remains lightweight, computationally efficient, and highly scalable across multiple microclimates. By dividing the system into distinct operational modules, the pipeline maintains a clean separation of concerns. For example, the forecasting models are trained independently of the battery dispatch logic, allowing different regressors to be swapped in as plug-and-play components without modifying the core physical equations of the simulation engine. This separation also enables independent testing and verification of each step, securing the overall structural integrity of the codebase.
High Level Module Diagram
The overall software design and data-flow transitions are visually illustrated in Figure 4.1. As delineated in the diagram, the software architecture is organized into five primary layers: the Ingestion Layer, the Modeling Layer, the Simulation Engine, the Orchestration and Quality Assurance Layer, and the Presentation Layer.

The data pipeline commences in the Ingestion Layer, where the raw multi-region weather records undergo geospatial coordinate filtering and gap-filling within the multi-region split module. The resulting cleaned meteorological traces are processed by the physical synthesis module to calculate the AC-side power generation profile, establishing the canonical target variable used consistently by the downstream modules.
Once the data is prepared, the flow bifurcates into the Modeling Layer and the Simulation Engine. In the Modeling Layer, the training module trains and hyper-tunes the Random Forest and Support Vector Regression models using historical training records, subsequently serializing the optimized models as persistent estimators. Simultaneously, the baseline simulation module within the Simulation Engine generates the deterministic, bimodal household load profiles and runs a stateful year-long simulation of the reactive self-consumption battery strategy, exporting the results as a baseline dataset.
The smart simulation module then integrates these baseline load profiles, the processed solar generation traces, and the serialized forecasting models. Every midnight, it applies a seeded forecast-error model to perturb weather features before calling the forecaster, estimating the day-ahead solar sum to activate Conservation Mode on low-solar days and executing the smart dispatch policy hourly.
The final output datasets from both baseline and smart runs are ingested by the Orchestration and Quality Assurance Layer. Here, the master controller manages the sensitivity sweeps, verifies the physical integrity of the energy logs against strict validation equations, and generates cross-region summaries. Structural correctness is further secured by the pytest regression suite. Finally, the Presentation Layer ingests the verified run results and serialized models to render the interactive Streamlit dashboard, providing researchers with scenario filters and live telemetry previews.

To maintain repository hygiene and ensure auditability, the project enforces a strict, configuration-driven directory structure that separates source code from generated files. The project root contains the core execution scripts, configuration files, and the canonical raw dataset. Implementation by Pipeline Phase
Phase 1: Multiple Region Ingestion and Cleaning
The raw meteorological dataset is ingested from the canonical swkdataweather.csv file, containing 219,360 total hourly records representing a five-year span (January 1, 2020 00:00 to January 1, 2025 23:00). The multi-region split module (phase1_split_multi_region.py) isolates divisional datasets by rounding geospatial coordinates to two decimal places and matching them against config-defined divisional centers within a strict tolerance limit of . This process isolates five identical divisional datasets containing exactly 43,872 hourly rows each.
To ensure continuous timeline integrity and handle missing data points, a strict gap-cleaning protocol is enforced. Each region's time index is localized to the state's timezone (Asia/Kuching) and resampled to a strict 1-hour frequency using pandas. Any missing data gaps introduced by sensor downtime or transmission lag are filled using a dual-method protocol. Numeric weather fields (e.g., temperature, humidity) are filled using linear interpolation with a strict limit of three consecutive hours to prevent over-smoothing. Non-numeric categorical fields (such as local weather conditions and source flags) are resolved using forward-filling and backward-filling, preventing silent failures and pandas dtype warnings. Finally, physical boundaries are enforced on radiative solar fields—Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI), Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI), Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI)—and precipitation by clipping negative sensor noise to a strict lower boundary of , securing the mathematical sanity of the physical models.
Phase 1b: Physical Synthesis
To synthesize the ground-truth AC-side power generation (Target_Power_kW), the processed meteorological fields are run through a physics-based pvlib ModelChain module (phase1b_physics_synthesis.py). To isolate the climatic impact of weather patterns in each region and eliminate hardware bias, the solar rooftop system parameters are strictly standardized across all five divisions. The physical setup consists of a canonical 5.0 kWp DC silicon array mounted with a fixed surface tilt of  and a south-facing azimuth of .
Cell temperature and thermal losses are calculated utilizing the Sandia Array Performance Model (SAPM) parameters for an open-rack glass-to-glass module configuration. The model chain calculates the physical angle of incidence (AOI) utilizing a localized solar position algorithm. The meteorological variables map to the internal physical models as follows: Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) maps to ghi, Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI) to dni, Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI) to dhi, and air temperature to temp_air. Wind speed is factored in at a constant default value of  where physical anemometer readings are missing. The resulting AC output is converted to kilowatts, clipped to zero, and exported as Target_Power_kW with timezone information stripped (tz-naive) to maintain pipeline consistency across the simulation engine.

Daily Reset Evaluation versus Continuous Run
The experimental methodology employed a dual-state evaluation approach to balance physical realism with statistical rigor. While the continuous simulation reflects realistic physical continuity and long-term battery cycling across the year, the statistical significance tests necessitated independent daily populations. Consequently, these specific evaluations utilized isolated daily reset states, forcing the battery to a default state of charge each midnight. A minor numerical discrepancy exists between the continuous yearly savings total and the straightforward summation of independent daily savings; this is an expected mathematical effect governed by the residual carry-over of stored energy that naturally occurs on non-reset days in the continuous timeline.

Discussion
Interpretation
The overarching interpretation of the experimental outcomes reveals that both the Random Forest and Support Vector Regression models possess extremely high explanatory power for predicting the target photovoltaic generation. The comparative results essentially reflect the foundational architectural tradeoff between the two algorithms: the Random Forest leverages the highly flexible, non-linear capabilities of ensemble decision trees to maximize overall savings, whereas the Support Vector Regression prioritizes robust margin constraints and computational stability. Both approaches successfully translate raw meteorological data into actionable financial leverage under the simulated Time-of-Use tariff structure.
Threats to Validity
Several inherent constraints must be recognized when interpreting the economic and operational findings of this simulation. Regarding the generation of synthetic loads, despite the injection of robust stochastic noise, real-world residential demand profiles feature distinct and highly unpredictable human behavioral spikes that parametric generation cannot fully replicate. Concerning the accuracy of the day-ahead lookahead, while the application of Gaussian noise to the historical weather features effectively simulates basic forecast uncertainty, it does not completely emulate the complex, compounding physical errors outputted by true numerical weather prediction centers. Finally, regarding the modeled economic policies, the cost calculation assumes strict zero financial compensation for any excess energy exported back to the grid, framing the algorithmic optimization purely as an import-reduction exercise rather than a holistic two-way retail billing simulation.

The testing follows a two-layer strategy designed to ensure both the structural integrity of the codebase and the mathematical correctness of the physical simulation. Layer A consists of 30 automated regression tests executed by pytest, grouped into data integrity for the raw swkdataweather.csv dataset, the correctness of the forecast noise injection (apply_forecast_noise), and the behavioural contracts of the core simulation (simulate). It should be noted that the data integrity test module is explicitly skipped if the swkdataweather.csv file is absent from the root directory. Layer B consists of hard systemic validations executed sequentially inside run_all.py after generating the baseline and smart operational outputs. These assertions enforce strict hourly regularity, State of Charge (SoC) limits, non-negative cost and import boundaries, energy balance within 1×10⁻⁶ kW, and tariff-cost consistency within 1×10⁻⁵ RM, as fully documented in the docs/VALIDATION.md reference. Together, these checks empirically demonstrate that the implementation conforms to the theoretical methodology proposed in Chapter 3.

Summary of the Project Work
Problem and Approach
This project addressed the challenge of optimizing grid-tied residential photovoltaic (PV) and battery systems under a Time-of-Use (ToU) tariff structure in Sarawak. The optimization targeted a ToU pricing model with peak hours from 07:00 to 22:00 charged at 0.245 RM/kWh and off-peak hours at 0.139 RM/kWh. The study compared a baseline reactive self-consumption controller against an AI-powered smart controller that utilizes a day-ahead "Conservation Mode". This smart policy relies on a daily critical energy threshold () defaulting to 18.0 kWh/day and restricts off-peak discharging if the State of Charge (SoC) falls below a reserve of 0.50. The study was executed as a multi-region simulation, not a field deployment, utilizing deterministic synthetic load generation (numpy_seed = 42) and PV generation synthesized via pvlib using historical weather data across five distinct Sarawak locations.
What was Built
A fully reproducible, configuration driven pipeline was developed, governed by a single experiment configuration JSON file. The implementation consisted of several strictly scripted phases.
For the data path, raw meteorological data from swkdataweather.csv was filtered and gap filled by the phase1_split_multi_region.py script, producing cleaned outputs that were converted into Target_Power_kW per region by the phase1b_physics_synthesis.py script.
Regarding the models, the phase3_ai_training.py module trained both Random Forest and a StandardScaler paired with a Support Vector Regression pipeline utilizing a TimeSeriesSplit of 5 and GridSearchCV. The dataset, comprising 43,872 hourly rows per region spanning from January 1, 2020 to January 1, 2025, was chronologically split at August 8, 2024, yielding 40,344 training rows and 3,528 held out test rows.
For system control, the phase4_smart_optimizer.py script simulated the battery dispatch logic and featured a forecast error module, named apply_forecast_noise, to inject realistic uncertainty, such as a default global horizontal irradiance  of 0.20, into day ahead predictions.
The orchestration was handled by the run_all.py script which executed the end to end pipeline, producing regional evaluation metrics, statistical tests including Wilcoxon and paired t tests, and automated multi seed sensitivity sweeps on both forecast noise and the  threshold.
Finally, for user interface and quality assurance, the findings were visualized via phase5_dashboard.py across four analytical tabs. Code integrity was strictly enforced by 30 automated pytest cases and continuous runtime validations checking energy balance and Time of Use billing identities.

Conclusions
Research Question and Hypothesis
The central hypothesis stated that predictive lookahead from an AI forecaster enables a day ahead operating mode that reduces grid electricity costs compared with myopic self consumption. This was proven correct under the tested parameters.
In terms of economic and operational impact, across all five simulated regions, the smart predictive dispatch successfully generated annual cost reductions and lowered peak grid imports. Specifically, Sri Aman achieved the highest total savings of RM 26.25 and the greatest peak import reduction of 404.09 kWh. Miri presented the most constrained optimization envelope, yielding RM 9.92 in savings via the Random Forest model.
Regarding algorithm comparison, the Random Forest algorithm consistently outperformed the Support Vector Regression model in both total monetary savings and peak energy reduction across all regions, while maintaining comparable test RMSE scores, such as the Kuching Random Forest Test RMSE of 0.043 compared to the Support Vector Regression score of 0.044.
For statistical significance, the observed daily cost reductions were statistically valid and not artifacts of random load variations, with all paired t test and Wilcoxon signed rank p values falling well below the 0.05 threshold. This is exemplified by the Kuching Random Forest Wilcoxon p value of 9.27 × 10⁻⁵⁰. The optimal critical energy threshold remained uniformly stable at 18.0 kWh for all regions.
Main Technical Contributions
The research yielded several main technical contributions. First, it delivered a reproducible multi region pipeline with a strictly auditable architecture controlled by a unified configuration file, successfully scaling from a single site prototype to five distinct Sarawak microclimates. Second, the study implemented dual transparent forecasters by comparing Random Forest and Support Vector Regression models utilizing time series aware cross validation. This avoided chronological leakage while maintaining high interpretability. Third, an explicit forecast error model closed the perfect foresight gap pervasive in simulation literature by introducing stochastic noise injection. This isolated the theoretical oracle bound where  equals 0 from realistic, noisy day ahead performance. Fourth, automated sensitivity analysis integrated continuous  sweeps and  multi seed aggregations to mathematically map the algorithms operational robustness against real world uncertainty. Finally, rigorous automated validation secured the digital twins physical integrity with a 30 case test suite and strict runtime energy balance assertions, ensuring findings are mathematically sound.
Objectives Mapping
The project successfully mapped its findings to the initial objectives. The first objective was to develop a multi region digital twin simulation framework integrating AI forecasting with a rule based controller. This was successfully achieved and implemented via phases 1 through 4, driven by the experiment configuration file, actively simulating 5 distinct Sarawak regions. The second objective was to evaluate economic and operational performance using statistical analyses and sensitivity sweeps, while delivering an interactive dashboard. This was also achieved, as statistical validity was verified via paired t tests, robustness was mapped via  and  sweeps, and the results were effectively visualized via a Streamlit interface.

Limitations
Data and Labels
The simulation relies on archived historical weather CSV files rather than a live Numerical Weather Prediction feed. Consequently, the forecast noise module acts as a stylized error model that simulates uncertainty, but it cannot perfectly emulate the complex physical errors generated by actual meteorological forecasting centers. Additionally, the photovoltaic generation truth is model generated via pvlib rather than sourced from physical inverter telemetry.
Load and Dwelling
Household energy demand was generated entirely synthetically. While parameterized with typical diurnal peaks and stochastic noise, these mathematically reproducible loads do not capture the unpredictable behavioral spikes and demographic diversity found in measured smart meter data from real Malaysian households.
Hardware and Tariff Generality
The system evaluated a single nominal hardware setup comprising a 5 kWp photovoltaic capacity, a 10 kWh battery, a 5 kW inverter limit, and a 0.95 round trip efficiency across all regions to strictly isolate the impact of local climate variability. Furthermore, the Time of Use economic model is a simplified framework assuming zero financial compensation for grid exported energy, thereby restricting the evaluation to an import reduction exercise rather than full retail bill modeling.
Machine Learning
While the forecasting models exhibited exceptionally high  values, this hourly accuracy can be misleading because the operational policy ultimately hinges on aggregate daily energy crossing the  threshold, not flawless curve tracking. Furthermore, due to computational scalability tradeoffs, the Support Vector Regression grid search was restricted to an 8,000 row chronologically spaced subsample before refitting to the full dataset.
Software Scope
The scope of this project is strictly confined to a digital twin software simulation. It does not incorporate the physical complexities of a localized Battery Management System, communication latency, grid compliance codes, or electrochemical cell degradation over time.

Future Work
To build upon the foundation established in this repository, several natural extensions are recommended. First, the integration of real load profiles could replace the synthetic load generator with measured smart meter datasets to validate the conservation logic against true stochastic human behavior. Second, archival day ahead forecast integration could substitute the synthetic noise injection model with actual archival day ahead numerical weather prediction data or established bias statistics to evaluate the algorithms under true meteorological prediction conditions. Third, lagged feature retraining is suggested to retrain the regressors strictly utilizing lagged or forecast era inputs, preventing the model from learning to invert same day physical constraints. Fourth, regarding hardware and tariff sweeps, the continuous evaluation orchestrator could be expanded to iterate through varying equipment sizes and dynamic feed in tariff scenarios. Fifth, algorithmic expansion could introduce additional algorithms, such as gradient boosting or lightweight neural networks, adhering to the established simulation pipeline contract to determine if more complex architectures yield tangible operational benefits. Finally, hardware in the loop piloting could bridge the gap between software simulation and field deployment by porting the predictive control logic onto embedded hardware integrated with a physical hybrid inverter for real time validation

Chapter Closing
This research successfully designed, validated, and evaluated a reproducible simulation framework for residential energy management in Sarawak. By demonstrating that forecast informed control logic can significantly reduce grid imports during expensive peak windows, the study establishes a mathematically verifiable baseline for smart energy dispatch under dynamic Time of Use structures. While constrained by its simulated nature and synthetic variables, the codebase proves that integrating interpretable AI with deterministic safety rules provides tangible economic benefits without the need for hardware expansion. The fully documented repository, configuration files, and automated testing artifacts stand as an open, robust foundation for future deployment and research.
